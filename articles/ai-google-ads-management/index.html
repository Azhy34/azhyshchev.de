AI Google Ads Management: How Custom Agent Tooling Stopped the Budget Leaks

A working case study in AI Google Ads management: a live e-commerce ad account across Google Ads and Pinterest, run day to day by an AI agent instead of through the dashboards. Most of the wasted budget here was never visible in the campaign view. It sat one level down, in the search queries nobody reads and the campaign settings nobody re-checks. This is where the money was going, why the first version of the automation reported a perfect score while the account kept losing it, and what had to change in the tooling to bring cost per click from 1.67 down to 0.50 EUR.

The business problem

Where ad budget actually leaks

When a campaign underperforms, the usual reaction is to change the bid, the budget or the creative. Those are the three levers the interface puts in front of you. The money, however, is usually leaving through a door that the campaign view does not show.

A 30 day audit of this account analysed 909 real search queries that had triggered ads. It surfaced three distinct leak patterns, none of which were visible at campaign level:

  • Wrong product intent. Shoppers searching for licensed cartoon characters the shop does not sell were clicking the ads and bouncing immediately. Every one of those clicks was paid for.
  • Wrong context. Queries for a completely different room type were matching the ads through broad keyword matching. Right category, wrong buyer.
  • Wrong buyer. Searches containing the words cheap and bargain were pulling in traffic that was never going to buy a premium product.

None of that shows up as a red flag in a campaign report. It shows up as a slightly worse cost per click and a slightly worse conversion rate, spread thinly across everything, which is exactly why it survives for months.

One keyword, 30.02 EUR, 18 clicks

A single broad match keyword in this account had spent 30.02 EUR across 18 clicks at an average cost per click of 1.67 EUR before it was identified and paused. That is one keyword out of hundreds. The point is not the absolute number, it is the rate: a leak like this scales linearly with your budget, and nothing in the standard reporting view flags it.

Why the first attempt failed

The automation reported 100 out of 100. The budget said otherwise.

The account is managed through an AI agent rather than by clicking through dashboards. The first version of that setup used an off-the-shelf connector to the Google Ads API: a standard, publicly available integration that exposes the advertising platform to an AI agent.

It worked, in the narrow sense that commands executed. Then a manual review of the account turned up a list of defects: ads running with a fraction of the required headline count, keywords duplicated across campaigns so that they competed against each other in the auction, and junk queries that had already spent money.

The agent had been reporting everything as fine. A perfect score, 100 out of 100.

It was not malfunctioning. It was answering honestly against the only definition of success it had been given: did the advertising platform accept the change. Nobody had told it that an ad with three headlines instead of fifteen gets a poor quality rating and therefore a more expensive click, or that two of your own campaigns holding the same keyword means you are bidding against yourself.

This is the failure mode that matters for any business adding AI to an operational process. The automation was optimising against the technical contract of the platform. The commercial contract, the rules that decide whether the money is well spent, was invisible to it.

The fix, in business terms

What you get when the tools know your rules

The tooling was rebuilt so that every action the agent can take validates against the business rules first. Four practical consequences:

Budget protection that runs on a schedule Search queries are mined automatically and classified into winners and waste. Junk terms are blocked as negative keywords before the next spend cycle, instead of being discovered in a quarterly review.
No silent misconfiguration An ad cannot go live below the quality standard, and a bid cannot be changed in a way that silently resets it. The guardrail sits inside the tool, so it holds even when the instruction was ambiguous.
One control surface across channels Google Ads and Pinterest return data in the same shape into one knowledge base with a shared keyword bank. Cross channel comparison stops being a manual export and merge job.
A human approval gate Every change to a live account is created in a paused state and waits for an explicit go. The agent proposes and prepares, a person decides what actually spends money.

Fit

Who this approach is for

This is not a fit for every advertiser. It pays off in a specific situation:

  • E-commerce and B2B companies spending consistently on paid search, where a percentage point of wasted spend is a real number every month rather than a rounding error.
  • Advertisers running more than one channel, where the reporting work of comparing Google Ads against Pinterest, Meta or another platform currently happens in spreadsheets.
  • Companies with no in-house PPC engineer, where the account is either managed part time by a marketer or handed to an agency with limited visibility into what changed and why.
  • Teams already experimenting with AI that want the operational safety rails before letting an agent near a live budget.

It is a poor fit for very small budgets, where the engineering effort will not pay for itself, and for accounts that need a fundamental strategy or positioning rethink first. Automation makes a sound strategy cheaper to execute. It does not rescue a broken one.

How it works under the hood

Everything above is the commercial result. What follows is the engineering that produced it, for readers who want to know whether the approach is sound rather than just whether the numbers look good.

Architecture

From a raw API wrapper to a custom MCP server

Both channels are exposed to the agent through MCP, the Model Context Protocol, which is the standard way to give an AI agent a defined set of tools. The question is not whether to use an MCP server. It is what level of abstraction its tools sit at.

An off-the-shelf MCP server mirrors the API: hundreds of low level methods, one per endpoint, each accepting the platform's native request objects. A facade exposes a small number of business shaped operations instead, and does the platform mechanics internally.

The replacement is two local Python MCP servers, a Google Ads MCP server and a Pinterest Ads MCP server:

  • google-ads-mcp, 18 tools over Google Ads API v23
  • pinterest-ads-mcp, 9 tools over Pinterest Ads API v5

The design rule is that the tool surface mirrors the real advertising hierarchy rather than the API surface. Each level exposes read tools and guarded write tools.

Campaign level
  ↓ audit, budget, targeting, bid ceiling, impression share
Ad group level
  ↓ keywords, negatives, collision detection across campaigns
Ad level
  ↓ creative validation, asset coverage, quality checklist
Every write path
  ↓ validate against business rules → create paused → wait for human go

Guardrails

What the tools refuse to do

The 0.01 EUR trap

In the Google Ads API, mutating a bid without explicitly populating the update mask does not raise an error. The platform accepts the call and silently resets the bid to its 0.01 EUR fallback, which effectively removes the keyword from the auction. This is exactly the kind of failure an agent cannot detect on its own, because the API returns success. The facade builds the field mask internally on every bid mutation, so the mistake cannot be made.

Creative standard as a gate, not a guideline

Responsive search ads perform best with the full complement of assets. The deployment tool enforces 15 headlines and 4 descriptions in the target language as a precondition. A partial ad is rejected at the tool boundary rather than published and flagged later.

Collision detection across campaigns

When a keyword appears in more than one active campaign, those campaigns enter the same auction and inflate each other's cost. A dedicated tool scans for exact and phrase overlaps and pauses the duplicates. In this account it paused 36 duplicated top keywords and brought active collisions to zero.

Human approval on anything that spends

Write tools create objects in a paused state by default. A new campaign, a new ad group, a bid change: all prepared, none live. This is deliberate, because the risk with a capable agent is not malice, it is a confidently executed misreading of an ambiguous instruction. A hypothetical question in a chat should never become a live change to a real budget.

The transferable principle

Design agent tools from business rules, not from API docs

The abstraction level of your tooling decides what your agent is allowed to be wrong about.

Give an agent raw endpoints and it will optimise against the contract those endpoints define, then report success in good faith. Encode the commercial rules into the tool surface and an entire class of expensive mistakes becomes structurally impossible rather than merely discouraged in a prompt.

This generalises well beyond advertising. Any operational system where an agent has write access, whether that is a CRM, an inventory platform or a billing system, has the same split between the technical contract and the business contract. The technical one is documented. The business one usually lives in someone's head, which is precisely why it needs to be written into the tools.

The same pattern applied to other platforms is covered in automating Google Cloud Console with MCP and in B2B lead generation via Google Maps MCP.

Adding another channel is now a matter of adding a server rather than rewriting the workflow. Google Analytics 4 and Meta Ads are the next two on the list.

FAQ

Common questions about AI-managed ad accounts

How do I stop wasting my Google Ads budget?
Most waste is invisible in the campaign dashboard because it hides one level down, in the actual search queries people typed. A 30 day audit of 909 real search queries on this account found three leak patterns: shoppers looking for licensed cartoon characters that the shop does not sell, people searching for a completely different room type, and bargain hunters searching for the word cheap. Each of those clicks was paid for and bounced. The fix is a recurring search term audit that classifies queries into winners and waste, then applies negative keywords automatically before the next spend cycle.
Can an AI agent manage my ad account safely?
Only if the tools it uses enforce your rules. An AI agent connected directly to the raw Google Ads API optimises for one thing: did the API accept the change. It will happily publish an ad with 3 headlines instead of 15 and report success. In this setup every write tool validates against business rules first, every mutation is created in a paused state, and nothing goes live in the account without a human approving it. The safety comes from the tool design, not from asking the model to be careful.
Why build a custom MCP server instead of using a ready-made one?
An off-the-shelf MCP server is a thin wrapper over the raw API. It exposes hundreds of low level methods that mirror API endpoints and knows nothing about your business: your quality standards, your promotions, your product range. A custom facade exposes a small number of business shaped tools instead, such as audit this campaign against our quality checklist or mine search terms and harvest negatives. The business rules live inside the tool, so a whole class of expensive mistakes becomes structurally impossible rather than merely discouraged.
Do I still need a PPC agency if I automate ad management with AI?
Automation replaces the mechanical part of the work: pulling reports, auditing search queries, spotting duplicate keywords across campaigns, checking that every ad meets the quality standard. It does not replace commercial judgement about positioning, offers and creative. What changes is where the human time goes. Instead of spending it on data collection and manual checks, it goes into strategy, while the agent handles the routine and flags anything that needs a decision.
